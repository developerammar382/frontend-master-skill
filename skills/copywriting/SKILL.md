# Copywriting

Generic copy makes good design look generic. Visitors spot AI copy in one line, and once they spot it they stop trusting the page.

## The test

Read every line out loud. If a friend who works at the company wouldn't say it in a conversation, rewrite it.

## Rules

1. **Be specific.** Name the thing, the number, the person. "Cuts invoice time from 3 hours to 20 minutes" beats "Streamline your workflow".
2. **Say one thing per sentence.** Short sentences. Vary the length so it doesn't sound like a metronome.
3. **Write to one reader.** "You", not "users" or "businesses".
4. **Lead with the outcome, then the feature.** People buy what changes for them.
5. **Use the customer's words.** If they say "invoices", don't say "billing artifacts". Pull language from reviews, support tickets, and sales calls when you have them.
6. **Cut every word that doesn't earn its place.** "In order to" becomes "to". "Very unique" becomes "unique", then ask whether it's unique at all.
7. **Prefer verbs to nouns.** "Compare plans" beats "Plan comparison".
8. **Don't promise what the product doesn't do.** Never invent stats, customers, or testimonials. Use clear placeholders like `[Customer quote]` and flag them.

## AI fingerprints (never use these)

Words: seamless, effortless, elevate, empower, unlock, unleash, supercharge, revolutionize, transform, cutting-edge, next-generation, game-changer, robust, leverage, harness, delve, dive into, navigate, landscape, realm, tapestry, journey, synergy, holistic, innovative, world-class, best-in-class, state-of-the-art.

Patterns:
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "Whether you're X or Y, we've got you covered."
- "It's not just X, it's Y."
- "Say goodbye to X. Say hello to Y."
- Three adjectives in a row: "fast, simple, and powerful"
- Rhetorical question headlines: "Ready to take your business to the next level?"
- Every feature card with the same sentence shape
- Em dashes to join clauses, and colons before every punchline
- Exclamation marks in product UI

## Writing each part

**Headline.** Under 10 words. A concrete outcome, a sharp claim, or a plain description of what it is. Plain beats clever when you're unsure.

**Subhead.** One or two lines that answer "how?" or "for whom?". Name the audience or the mechanism.

**Buttons.** Verb plus object: "Create project", "Book a demo", "Download the report". Never "Click here", "Submit", or "Learn more" alone.

**Feature blocks.** Title says the benefit in 2-5 words. Body says how in one or two sentences. Vary the shape between blocks.

**Social proof.** Real names, roles, companies, and a specific result. "Cut our onboarding time in half" from a named person beats "Great product!" from "John D."

**Empty states.** Say what goes here, why it's empty, and the one action that fills it.

**Errors.** Say what happened, why if you know, and how to fix it. Never blame the user. No "Oops".

**Forms.** Labels are nouns ("Work email"). Helper text explains why you need it. Validation messages say how to fix the input.

**Microcopy.** Loading: say what's loading ("Loading invoices"). Success: confirm the specific thing ("Invoice sent to Dana").

## Voice

Pick a voice in the design brief, in three words, and match it everywhere:

| Product | Voice |
| --- | --- |
| Dev tool | Direct, precise, a little dry |
| Finance | Calm, exact, reassuring |
| Consumer app | Warm, quick, a bit playful |
| Luxury | Spare, confident, unhurried |
| Health | Kind, clear, never alarming |

If the brand already has a voice, use it. Your taste comes second.

## Checklist

- [ ] No word from the fingerprint list
- [ ] Headline under 10 words and specific to this product
- [ ] Every button is verb plus object
- [ ] No invented stats, logos, or quotes; placeholders are flagged
- [ ] Empty, error, and loading states have real copy
- [ ] Sentence length varies; feature blocks don't share one shape
- [ ] Read aloud once. Nothing sounds like a brochure.
